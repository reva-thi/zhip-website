What We Do

Eight programmes.
One community.
One mission.

Every ZHIP programme is designed to do two things at once: solve an immediate problem and build the long-term capacity that makes the immediate problem less likely to recur. We feed people today and we build the knowledge and infrastructure that means fewer people need feeding tomorrow.

How it actually works

What a family experiences when they find ZHIP.

The eight programmes are not separate initiatives. They are connected stages in a journey that most families take — from needing food support, to learning to cook, to growing food, to contributing to others. This is what that journey looks like in practice.

Month 1

A family finds the hub

A mother hears about the ZHIP hub through her child's school. She comes with two children on Wednesday afternoon to the food pantry. She is welcomed by name by a volunteer she has never met. She chooses food from the display — rice, lentils, tinned tomatoes, a bunch of spinach, some spices. She takes a recipe card from the exit table. No means test. No forms. Just food, and a friendly face.

Month 2

She receives a family food box

The pantry coordinator mentions the family food box programme — a monthly kit delivered to the home with a week of whole food ingredients, seeds, and a ZHIP Academy access card. She signs up. The box arrives the following Monday. The seeds go on the window sill. The recipe cards go on the fridge. Her daughter aged nine plants the coriander seeds and waters them every morning.

Month 3

She joins the Thursday cooking class

The ZHIP Academy Foundation course starts at the hub on Thursday evenings. The volunteer who gave her the food box tells her about it. She goes. There are eight people — one of them speaks the same language she grew up speaking. The trainer demonstrates how to make a proper dal — the first dish they cook together uses the lentils from her food box. She goes home that night and makes it for her children. They eat it. They want more.

Month 5

Her child joins the children's growing programme

The hub's school programme includes a growing club on Saturday mornings. Her daughter joins. She is given a plot — a one-metre raised bed — and seeds chosen from the seed library. By month six she has harvested her first tomatoes. She brings them home and shows her mother. Her mother cooks them with oil and garlic, the first thing she learned in the Thursday class. They eat them together.

Month 8

She becomes a volunteer

Eight months after first walking through the pantry door, the mother is confident in the kitchen. The Hub Coordinator notices and asks if she would consider volunteering on Tuesday kitchen days. She starts as a prep volunteer — washing vegetables, measuring ingredients — and within three sessions is cooking alongside the kitchen lead. She brings her children. They help serve. The family that came to receive is now contributing.

Month 12

She starts a small food enterprise

The mother has been making a traditional fermented condiment her grandmother taught her, using a recipe from her home country. Friends and other hub members ask her to make it for them. The Hub Coordinator introduces her to the food enterprise module of the Academy and to the Saturday community market. She sells her first jars. Within three months she has regular customers and a small income. The family has gone from food insecurity to food sovereignty in twelve months.

This is not a case study. This is the pattern we design for — and that we see happen, in different forms and at different speeds, in every community where ZHIP takes root. The eight programmes are the stages of this journey. The hub is the place where it happens.

The eight programmes

Each one a stage. All eight a journey.

01Community Hot Kitchens

Hot, nutritious, culturally appropriate meals served in community spaces by volunteer teams, two to three times per week. Not a soup kitchen. A dining room where the whole community is welcome, nobody pays unless they can, and everyone who wants to learn is invited to cook.

In the hub: The community kitchen is the physical and social heart of the hub — the programme that meets the most people and opens the door to everything else. Every session is also a food skills teaching session for volunteers.

Full Detail
02Family Food Security Kits

Monthly boxes of whole nutritious food — lentils, grains, produce, spices, seeds, and recipe cards — delivered or collected by food-insecure families. Every kit also includes an Academy access card and seeds for growing at home.

In the hub: Kits are assembled monthly by hub volunteers, incorporating rescued food and seasonal produce from the growing programme. They are the first point of contact for many families — and the springboard to everything else the hub offers.

Full Detail
03Food Skills Academy

Weekly in-person cooking and food knowledge classes in hub spaces, taught by certified community instructors. Ten subject areas, four progression levels, digital support between sessions, and real certifications on completion.

In the hub: The Academy is not separate from the hub's other programmes — it is the educational spine that runs through all of them. The kitchen is the classroom, the garden is the classroom, the pantry is the classroom.

Full Detail
04Growing & Seed Libraries

Community gardens, household growing support, and community seed libraries where members borrow seeds, grow food, and return seeds at harvest. The surplus harvest feeds the community kitchen. The seed library grows every year.

In the hub: The growing programme connects directly to the kitchen (surplus harvest) and the Academy (growing skills module). Children in the growing club often become the most engaged Academy learners — and the most enthusiastic about food.

Full Detail
05Elder Nourishment

Daily hot meal delivery to isolated elderly people in the community — with a human visit, not just a food drop. Weekly communal elder lunches where elders are invited to co-teach their food traditions to younger members.

In the hub: The elder programme is where the Academy most explicitly honours traditional knowledge. Elders who can no longer cook are the community's most valuable food knowledge resource. The hub creates the conditions for that knowledge to be shared before it is lost.

Get Involved
06Children's Nutrition First

Breakfast and lunch programmes in schools and holiday clubs, children's growing clubs, and monthly parent nutrition workshops. Ensuring children have nutritious food year-round — and that their parents have the knowledge to sustain good nutrition at home.

In the hub: At school hubs, children and parents learn together — with children often teaching parents what they have discovered in the growing club or cooking session. The knowledge travels from school to household in ways that conventional parent education rarely achieves.

Full Detail
07Emergency Food Response

Rapid food deployment when a community faces acute crisis — natural disaster, economic shock, sudden displacement. Pre-positioned food stores, a volunteer rapid-response network, mobile kitchen capability, and a pathway back to regular programme support after the crisis.

In the hub: Hubs that are already running regular programmes have the volunteer relationships, the food rescue connections, and the community trust that make emergency response possible at speed. A hub is resilience infrastructure — even before a crisis happens.

08Food Rescue & Zero Waste

Collecting surplus edible food from supermarkets, restaurants, caterers, and farms — and redirecting it to community kitchens, pantries, and families before it reaches landfill. Building the local logistics, volunteer network, and food rescue partnerships that make this work at neighbourhood scale.

In the hub: Food rescue feeds the kitchen and the pantry — reducing costs and increasing the volume of food available for distribution. The Academy's zero waste module connects directly to this programme, teaching learners how to cook with whatever is rescued on any given day.
