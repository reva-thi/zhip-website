ZHIP Food Skills Academy

Not an online course.
A community of people
learning to feed themselves.

The ZHIP Food Skills Academy is a structured, certified, community-led food education system — delivered in the kitchens, gardens, and gathering spaces of ZHIP community hubs, supported by digital resources, and built around one core conviction: food knowledge should belong to everyone.

Why the Academy exists

The knowledge that used to be passed at the kitchen table.

Three generations ago, most families had someone in them who could cook a nourishing meal from whatever was in season, preserve food for winter, tend a kitchen garden, and feed a household on a modest budget. That knowledge was transmitted informally — at the kitchen table, in the garden, at the market. It was not a curriculum. It was culture.

That chain of transmission broke. Through urbanisation, through the rise of ultra-processed food, through generations who did not have time or space or someone to teach them. Today, the food knowledge that would most directly reduce food insecurity — affordable cooking, growing, preservation, budgeting — is the knowledge most communities have least access to.

The ZHIP Food Skills Academy restores that chain of transmission. Not by putting people in front of a screen and sending them a quiz. By gathering them in a kitchen together, giving them knives and vegetables and a certified community instructor, and letting the learning happen the way it always has — through making, tasting, sharing, and doing again.

"The Academy did not teach me recipes. It taught me how to cook. Those are completely different things. A recipe is a script. Cooking is understanding — of flavour, of timing, of what food needs. I now know what food needs."

— Foundation Academy Graduate, Houston

"I had been afraid of my kitchen for forty years. Six weeks in the Thursday class and that changed. I made dal from scratch on week three and I have not bought a ready meal since."

— Foundation Academy Graduate, Dallas
How it is structured

In-person learning. Digital support. Community certification.

🧑‍🍳

In-person community sessions

The core of the Academy is in-person — people cooking together in a hub kitchen under the guidance of a trained community instructor. Sessions run for two hours, once a week, over six to eight weeks per subject module. Classes are kept small (8–15 people) so the instructor can guide each person's hands-on practice.

No professional kitchen required. Any space with a hob, running water, and a work surface can run Academy sessions.

📱

Digital resources between sessions

Between weekly sessions, learners access supporting digital content — short video demonstrations, illustrated guides, and downloadable recipe cards — on any smartphone. They try things at home, note what worked and what did not, and bring questions to the next session.

Digital content works offline after first download — essential for learners in low-connectivity areas. Available in 20+ languages.

🏅

Practical certification

Certification in the Academy is skills-based, not exam-based. The instructor observes each learner preparing a dish or demonstrating a skill — and certifies what they can actually do, not what they can recall on paper. Digital certificates are issued to each learner's ZHIP profile, permanently and portably.

Certificates are verifiable by employers, food safety regulators, and other organisations recognising ZHIP credentials.

The teaching method

Watch. Do. Teach. The three-layer approach.

The Academy teaches the same way food knowledge has always been transmitted best — not through instruction, but through example, practice, and eventually, teaching others. Every learner moves through three stages, at their own pace.

1

Watch

The instructor demonstrates the technique first — not while explaining it, but while doing it. Learners watch the whole technique before any explanation begins. They see what the food looks like at each stage, smell when something is ready, hear when oil is at the right temperature. Then the instructor explains what they did and why.

Research consistently shows that demonstration-first teaching produces better retention than instruction-first. The Academy is built around this principle throughout.

2

Do

Every learner makes the dish themselves. In pairs where space requires it, but as individually as possible. The instructor's hands do not touch the learner's food — the instructor's voice guides. If something goes wrong, it is corrected, not taken over. The rule is absolute: the learner's hands do the cooking.

The mistakes that happen in the doing are not failures. They are the most important learning events in the session. An overcooked onion teaches more about heat than ten minutes of instruction.

3

Teach

Advanced learners are invited to assist newer learners — to explain what they know to someone who is still learning it. This is the stage at which knowledge becomes truly owned. Advanced learners who wish to pursue trainer certification enter the Train the Trainer programme, which prepares them to lead their own cohorts.

The trainer who teaches at a hub is almost always someone who started in the same hub as a Foundation learner. The knowledge circulates within the community that generated it.

Inside a typical session

A Thursday evening class, week by week.

Here is what a six-week Foundation cooking cohort looks like, from the first session to the last — in a community kitchen, with eight adults who came not knowing quite what to expect.

WEEK 1

The first night — knives, heat, and salt

Eight adults arrive. Some have never cooked anything more than toast. The instructor begins not with a recipe but with a question: what did your mother or grandmother make that you wish you could make yourself? The answers fill a board. The rest of the course will address some of them. Tonight: how to hold a knife safely, how to dice an onion without crying, how to heat oil correctly, and how to season. They make a simple pan-cooked onion and tomato sauce. They eat it together on bread. They go home with a printed guide and one question to answer before next week: what is your family's most important dish?

WEEK 2

Grains and legumes — the global foundation

The instructor introduces lentils, chickpeas, rice, oats, and quinoa — and tells the story of each: where they come from, how many billions of people they have fed, what makes them nutritionally extraordinary. Each participant makes one dish: red lentil dal, seasoned with the spices of their choosing. The instructor moves between pairs, guiding without taking over. They eat together. The learner who arrived last week saying "I don't really cook" has made a meal that surprises her with its flavour.

WEEK 3

Vegetables — what to do with everything in season

Tonight's ingredients come from this week's food rescue donation: a glut of sweet potatoes, some ageing spinach, and a bag of tomatoes. The session becomes a practical lesson in improvisation — how to make a nutritious, satisfying meal from whatever is available. This is the most important lesson in the whole course, and the instructor names it: you will not always have the right ingredient. Learning to work with what you have is the real skill. They make a roasted sweet potato and spinach tray bake. It is the best meal most of them have eaten at the hub.

WEEK 4

Flavour — spice, acid, fat, salt, and time

The instructor presents five bowls: one dish seasoned with no salt, one with no acid, one with no fat, one with no spice, one with all four. Each is tasted in order. The session becomes a sensory education in how flavour works — what each element does, how they combine, why balance matters. Participants make two versions of the same dish — one under-seasoned, one adjusted — and taste the difference. From this session forward, they no longer follow a recipe for seasoning. They taste and adjust.

WEEK 5

Preservation — making abundance last

The community garden had a glut of cucumbers this week. Tonight's session: quick pickling, fermentation basics, and making a simple chutney. Each participant goes home with two jars they made themselves — one pickled, one fermented. The instructor explains what fermentation does to food: how it increases digestibility, creates probiotics, and has been used in every food culture on earth for thousands of years. Half the group asks about continuing to the fermentation Advanced module.

WEEK 6

The graduation meal — you cook, I eat

The final session inverts the usual dynamic. Each participant plans and cooks one dish — any dish they choose — while the instructor watches. No guidance unless asked. Afterwards they eat together, and each person describes what they made, why they chose it, and what they would do differently. The instructor assesses their skills against the Foundation checklist and signs their certificate. The certificate is in their ZHIP profile within the hour. Six weeks ago, half of them could not dice an onion.

Ten subject areas

What the Academy teaches — and how each subject is delivered in a hub.

SUBJECT 1

🔪 Kitchen Foundations & Food Safety

Knife skills, heat control, kitchen hygiene, food storage, the ten foundational cooking techniques, and reading a recipe. The starting module for every Foundation learner.

How it is taught in a hub: Entirely hands-on in the hub kitchen. Every skill is demonstrated then practised immediately. Food safety assessment is practical — learners demonstrate correct storage and hygiene, not describe it. The hub kitchen is the classroom. There is no other classroom for this module.

SUBJECT 2

🌿 Nutrition & Food as Medicine

Macronutrients, micronutrients, reading food labels, healing spices (turmeric, ginger, moringa, black seed, fenugreek), gut health, anti-inflammatory eating, and traditional healing diets across cultures.

How it is taught in a hub: Each session pairs a nutritional concept with a practical cooking demonstration — so the science is always embodied in a dish. A session on anti-inflammatory eating ends with the group cooking a golden lentil soup. A session on gut health ends with making a simple yoghurt. At healthcare hubs, clinical staff co-deliver this module with ZHIP trainers.

SUBJECT 3

🌍 Plant-Based & Cultural Cooking

Plant-centred cooking across eight global food traditions — South Asian, West African, East Asian, Middle Eastern, Latin American, Mediterranean, Nordic, and Ethiopian. Cultural food heritage documentation and recovery.

How it is taught in a hub: Each session explores one food culture — its foundational ingredients, flavour principles, and most accessible dishes. Elders from each tradition are invited as guest teachers. At faith hubs and neighbourhood hubs, the instructor adapts the curriculum to the specific cultural mix of the cohort, ensuring every learner sees their own food heritage valued and taught.

SUBJECT 4

🌱 Food Growing & Garden Skills

Container gardening, raised beds, soil health, seed starting, pest management, composting, seed saving, and seasonal planning. From window sill to community plot.

How it is taught in a hub: This module is delivered entirely in the hub's growing space — whether that is a community garden, a school plot, or a collection of containers on a roof terrace. Sessions are seasonal — what is being planted, tended, or harvested at each point in the growing year determines what is taught. The seed library is introduced in this module, and seed saving is practised at the end of the growing season.

SUBJECT 5

🫙 Food Preservation & Fermentation

Pickling, jamming, dehydrating, freezing, fermentation science and safety, yoghurt, kefir, kimchi, kombucha, sourdough, miso, and traditional fermented foods from diverse cultures.

How it is taught in a hub: Preservation is taught in close connection with the growing programme — sessions are scheduled around seasonal gluts. When the garden produces more courgettes than the kitchen can use fresh, that week's preservation session processes them into pickles and chutney. Every learner goes home with jars they made. At food enterprise hubs, the fermentation module directly feeds the community market — learners' kimchi and kombucha become products they sell.

SUBJECT 6

♻️ Zero Waste Cooking

Eliminating food waste at home and in community kitchens — meal planning, creative use of every ingredient, vegetable scrap stocks, composting, and understanding the food system that creates waste.

How it is taught in a hub: Every community kitchen session models zero waste practice — the kitchen lead demonstrates using carrot tops in stock, stale bread as croutons, overripe bananas in porridge. The zero waste module formalises what is already modelled. Learners audit their own household waste for one week and bring the data to the session. The composting system at the hub is always part of the practical teaching.

SUBJECT 7

💼 Food Entrepreneurship

Food business basics, food safety registration, recipe costing, pricing, packaging, market selling, cooperative enterprise models, and social enterprise development.

How it is taught in a hub: At food enterprise hubs, this module runs in the commercial kitchen with real products and a real market as the practical assessment. At community centre hubs, the Saturday market is the assessment environment. Learners produce a product, cost it, price it, package it, and sell it — with a mentor alongside for their first market day. At faith hubs, the interfaith feast is the catering enterprise exercise.

SUBJECT 8

👨‍👩‍👧 Children & Family Nutrition

Nutritional needs across childhood, budget family meal planning, cooking for children, healthy lunchboxes, engaging children in food, and teaching food to young people.

How it is taught in a hub: At school hubs, children co-teach their parents in the monthly family sessions — a reversal that both generations find memorable. At community centre hubs, family cooking nights involve parents and children cooking together. The module explicitly teaches parents how to involve children in kitchen tasks at each age — because a six-year-old washing vegetables is as important as an adult learning to dice one.

SUBJECT 9

🏠 Running Community Food Programmes

How to run a food pantry, a community kitchen, a food rescue operation, or a seed library — programme design, volunteer coordination, dignity-first service, impact recording, and fundraising.

How it is taught in a hub: This module is delivered through apprenticeship within the hub itself — learners shadow and then lead elements of real programme sessions, under the supervision of experienced coordinators. The assessment is practical: planning and leading one session of a real community kitchen or pantry distribution, evaluated by the Hub Coordinator.

SUBJECT 10

🧘 Mindful Eating & Food as Culture

Eating with awareness and gratitude, the gut-brain connection, food traditions and spiritual practice across cultures, communal eating as community building, and the ethics of food choice.

How it is taught in a hub: At faith hubs, this module is the most natural — connecting food practice to spiritual practice already embedded in the community. At all hubs, each session begins with a moment of awareness before eating — a brief, inclusive, non-religious acknowledgement of the journey the food has made. Elders are invited to share food memories and cultural food rituals. The module ends with the cohort designing and hosting a communal celebration meal together.

Becoming a Trainer

The knowledge stays in the community
because the trainer stays in the community.

The most important design decision in the ZHIP Academy is that the trainers who deliver it are members of the communities they teach. Not outside experts who visit. Not professionals from somewhere else. Neighbours who have learned, practised, certified, and are now sharing what they know with the people who live and eat alongside them.

This matters because it changes the nature of what is transmitted. A neighbour teaching their own community's food culture, in the community's own language, using ingredients from the community's food tradition, is not just delivering a curriculum. They are reweaving the fabric of cultural food knowledge that was broken across generations.

The Train the Trainer programme takes experienced Academy learners (Harvest level or above) and prepares them to lead their own cohorts. It is not an academic qualification. It is a practical apprenticeship — alongside an experienced trainer, in a real hub, with a real cohort watching the apprentice trainer grow.

What a community trainer does

📅

Plans and delivers six-to-eight-week cohorts in their chosen subject area, in their hub, with their community

🌍

Adapts the ZHIP curriculum to the specific cultural context of their cohort — the ingredients, languages, and food traditions of the people they are teaching

📋

Assesses learners' practical skills and issues ZHIP certificates through the hub management platform

🤝

Participates in the monthly ZHIP trainer network — sharing learning, troubleshooting challenges, and receiving ongoing professional development

💰

Earns income from delivering sessions to organisations, healthcare partners, and other hubs on a fee-for-service basis — the Academy creates a livelihood as well as sharing knowledge

Six trainer tracks — choose the one that fits your expertise

🍳 Community Kitchen Trainer

Leads in-person cooking sessions in community kitchen settings. For people who love to cook and want to share that love with their community.

🧒 Children & Youth Educator

Teaches food skills to children ages 4–18 in school and community settings. Requires DBS/safeguarding check. For people who know how to work with young people.

🏥 Healthcare & Wellness Trainer

Integrates food knowledge into clinical and community health settings. For healthcare professionals and community health workers.

🕌 Faith & Community Elder Trainer

Delivers food education within faith and cultural community contexts, integrating spiritual and food traditions. For faith leaders and respected community elders.

💼 Food Enterprise Trainer

Supports aspiring food entrepreneurs through business development — from kitchen to market. For people with food business experience who want to share it.

🏛️ Community Organiser Trainer

Builds community food sovereignty infrastructure and advocacy capacity. For experienced community organisers and development workers.

Ready to become a trainer?

You need to have completed at least the Harvest level of the Academy in your chosen subject area, have three or more months of experience in a ZHIP hub, and be recommended by your Hub Coordinator. Applications are reviewed quarterly.
