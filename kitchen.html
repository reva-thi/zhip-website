Programme 1

Community Hot Kitchens

A ZHIP Community Kitchen is a regular hot meal service run by neighbours, for neighbours — in a local space that already exists. Not a soup kitchen. Not a food bank. A table where the whole community is welcome, nobody pays unless they can, and everyone who wants to cook is invited to learn.

🍲

25–60 meals per session

A typical kitchen serves between 25 and 60 people per session. Bigger is possible with more volunteers and a larger space.

📅

2–3 sessions per week

Most community kitchens run two or three sessions per week. Some run every day. You start with what your volunteer team can sustain.

👥

4–8 volunteers per session

A session runs with as few as four volunteers: one kitchen lead, two food preparation volunteers, and one service volunteer.

A day in the life

What actually happens on a Tuesday.

Here is what a typical ZHIP community kitchen session looks like — from the moment the first volunteer arrives to the last bowl being washed.

8am

Food arrives and is assessed

Rescued food collected the previous day from a local supermarket or restaurant partner arrives at the kitchen space. The kitchen lead and one volunteer sort through it: what is in good condition, what needs using today, what can be stored. This shapes the day's menu.

9am

Volunteers arrive. Roles assigned.

The full volunteer team arrives. Roles for the session are assigned: prep, cooking, service, and welcome. New volunteers are paired with experienced ones. The kitchen lead runs a five-minute briefing — today's menu, any allergens to flag, how many guests we are expecting, and any guests with specific needs from last week.

9:30am

Preparation begins

Vegetables are washed and chopped. Grains are measured and started. Sauces are prepared. The kitchen lead demonstrates any technique that is new or unfamiliar — this is also when cooking education happens naturally, without it feeling like a class. The conversation over the chopping board is half the point.

10:30am

Main cooking

Today's menu: a West African groundnut stew with rice and a fresh green salad. Enough for 45 people. The recipe is pinned to the wall in the ZHIP standard format — quantities scaled for the session size, cultural notes included, allergens clearly marked. The smell fills the building.

12pm

Service begins

Tables are set. The space looks like a dining room, not a charity hall. Guests arrive — some regular faces, some new. Each person is greeted by name where the volunteer knows them, or by a warm welcome if they are new. Meals are served at the table. Nobody queues with a tray. People eat together. Conversation happens. A child helps carry water. An elder tells a story about the food they ate growing up.

1:30pm

Service ends. Surplus packaged.

Remaining food is portioned into containers and given to guests who want to take food home, or set aside for the two elderly neighbours who could not come today and will receive a delivered meal. Nothing goes to waste.

2pm

Clean-up and debrief

Kitchen cleaned to food safety standard. Volunteer hours recorded in the ZHIP app. Meals served logged. Food rescue quantities noted. Any guest welfare concerns shared confidentially with the hub coordinator. Next week's menu roughed out based on what food rescue items are likely to come in.

2:30pm

Impact recorded automatically

The ZHIP platform calculates: meals served, volunteers hours given, food rescued and used, estimated CO2 diverted from landfill, and updates the hub's real-time impact dashboard. The data feeds into the quarterly community impact report that goes to the hub's supporters and funders.

The dignity principles

What makes a ZHIP kitchen different.

🪑 Tables, not queues

Guests sit at set tables and are served. They do not stand in a line with a tray. The physical experience of the meal communicates that this is a dining room — not a food station. This is a deliberate design choice, and it changes how people feel about being there.

🌍 Culturally appropriate menus

The menu rotates through the food traditions of the community being served. A kitchen serving a predominantly West African and South Asian neighbourhood will cook groundnut stew, jollof rice, dal, and sabzi — not generic "community cooking." Food is identity. We take that seriously.

🤝 Everyone is welcome

ZHIP kitchens do not check eligibility. They do not ask for proof of income. They do not have a means test. Anyone who comes to the table is welcome at the table. A suggested donation model means those who can contribute do — and those who cannot are never asked.

📚 Every session teaches something

Volunteers learn to cook — intentionally. The kitchen lead teaches one technique, one ingredient, or one food story at every session. Guests who want to learn are invited into the kitchen. The meal service is also always a food education session, for whoever wants it.

What you need to start

Starting a community kitchen — step by step.

What you need to provide

🏠

A kitchen space — a faith community hall, community centre, school kitchen, or any space with basic cooking facilities. The space does not need to be commercial grade to start. ZHIP will advise on what is needed.

👥

A core volunteer team — a minimum of four committed people who can show up consistently. You do not need trained chefs. You need people willing to learn and willing to show up.

🤝

Community relationships — you know your neighbourhood. You know who needs a hot meal. That local knowledge is irreplaceable, and it is the most important thing you bring to the partnership.

📋

A willingness to be accountable — ZHIP is transparent about impact. Hub coordinators commit to recording meals served, volunteer hours, and food handled so the wider community can see the impact of their support.

What ZHIP provides

🗃️

The Kitchen Launch Kit — equipment starter pack, recipe library (15 culturally diverse recipes that serve 25, with ingredient lists and cooking guides), menu planning calendar, service layout guide, and volunteer coordination materials.

📱

The Hub Management App — free digital tools for volunteer scheduling, meal tracking, inventory management, food safety records, and impact reporting. Accessible on any smartphone. Works offline.

🍎

Food rescue partnerships — ZHIP connects your kitchen to local supermarket and restaurant food rescue programmes, reducing your ingredient costs significantly and preventing good food from going to landfill.

👨‍🏫

Training and support — your kitchen lead receives food safety and community kitchen management training before the first session. Ongoing support from a ZHIP programme advisor throughout your first year.

💰

Access to grant funding — registered ZHIP community kitchens gain access to our grant programme, connecting them to USDA SNAP-Ed, community food system grants, and ZHIP's own Community Food Fund.
