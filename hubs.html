Community Hubs

What a ZHIP Hub means
for a neighbourhood.

A hub is the place where your community decides that hunger is not acceptable, isolation is not inevitable, and food knowledge belongs to everyone — and then does something about it. Together.

Start a Hub in Your Community See Hub Types
The deeper meaning

What changes when a community has a hub.

🏡

Strangers become neighbours

Before the hub opened, the families on the same street had never spoken. Within six months of a Tuesday kitchen starting, they knew each other by name, cooked for each other's children, and helped when someone was in hospital. The kitchen was the reason they started talking. Food is one of the oldest reasons human beings have ever gathered together — and when it works, it still works.

📚

Knowledge stays in the community

A certified ZHIP trainer from the community teaches classes in the community language, using ingredients from the community's food culture, in a space that belongs to the community. The knowledge does not travel somewhere else after the class ends. It stays in the hands of the people who live there — and they pass it on. A grandmother who joins the Thursday class teaches her recipe to the trainer. The trainer adds it to the curriculum. The knowledge circulates, deepens, and grows richer every season.

💰

Money circulates locally

Before the hub: a family buys food at a national supermarket chain. The money leaves the neighbourhood. After the hub: the same family buys fresh produce from the community garden, sells fermented goods at the Saturday market, pays a ZHIP-certified trainer for a cooking course. The money moves between neighbours. It multiplies locally. The research is consistent: every dollar spent in a local community food economy circulates at three times the rate of the same dollar spent at a chain retailer.

🧓

Elders are honoured, not forgotten

The elder who has never left her house since her husband died gets a hot meal delivered on Tuesday — and a volunteer who stays for twenty minutes and knows her name. At the monthly feast, she is invited to teach her traditional recipe to a group of young people who have never tasted it. She becomes, in that moment, a teacher and a holder of irreplaceable knowledge. The hub gave her that. It gave it to them too.

👧

Children grow up differently

The child who plants a seed at age six, harvests a tomato at age seven, makes a salsa with it at age eight, and sells jam at the community market at age twelve is not just a child who learned to cook. She is a child who understands where food comes from, who has experienced the satisfaction of growing something, who knows that her contribution matters to people she can name. That child grows into an adult with a fundamentally different relationship with food — and with community.

🛡️

The community becomes resilient

When the pandemic hit, the communities with food networks — with established relationships between neighbours, with stored knowledge of how to cook, with community gardens and seed libraries — fared better. Not just economically, though that too. They fared better in terms of isolation, mental health, and their ability to look after each other. A ZHIP hub is, among other things, a resilience investment. Communities that gather around food have something to fall back on that no government programme can manufacture from the outside.

Six types of community hub

Every community space can become a hub.
Each one runs the programmes that make sense for its context.

A hub is not a one-size-fits-all model. A faith community hub runs different programmes than a school hub. A healthcare hub serves different needs than a community centre. What makes them all ZHIP hubs is the same values, the same quality of care, and the same connection to the ZHIP Food Academy and wider network.

Hub Type 1

🕌 The Faith Community Hub

Faith communities — churches, mosques, temples, gurdwaras, synagogues — are among the most trusted institutions in most neighbourhoods. They already have kitchens, halls, and a community of people committed to service. A ZHIP faith hub turns that existing infrastructure and trust into a food sovereignty engine.

What makes faith hubs distinctive is the integration of food with spiritual life — the understanding, present in every major tradition, that feeding people is an act of worship and that the table is a sacred space. ZHIP programmes are designed to deepen that understanding rather than replace it.

"Our kitchen was already there. Our congregation was already committed to service. ZHIP gave us the structure, the training, and the network to turn what we were already doing into something systematic and measurable."

— Faith Hub Coordinator, Houston

Community programmes at a Faith Hub

EVERY WEEK
Sunday Community Meal

A hot meal served to all after the main service — open to congregation members and any neighbour who walks in. Cooked by a rotating team of congregation volunteers trained by the ZHIP kitchen lead. The meal rotates culturally each week, celebrating the food traditions of the congregation's diverse membership.

EVERY WEEK
Food Pantry for Members in Need

A quiet, dignity-first food distribution available to congregation members and wider community members experiencing hardship. Run twice weekly, staffed by trained volunteers, stocked through food rescue and congregation donations.

WEEKLY — ZHIP ACADEMY
Thursday Evening Cooking Class

Six-week Foundation cooking courses run in the faith kitchen, taught by a ZHIP-certified trainer from the congregation. Open to all — congregation members, neighbours, and anyone the community invites in. Uses the ZHIP curriculum adapted to the religious dietary practices of the congregation.

MONTHLY
Interfaith Cultural Food Exchange

Monthly communal dinners where members bring dishes from their family's food heritage and share the story behind them. Elders are honoured guests. Young people document the recipes. A living archive of the community's food culture is built over time.

SEASONAL
Festival and Fasting Calendar Integration

ZHIP supports faith hubs in running food programmes aligned with their religious calendar — Ramadan iftar meals, Navratri feast weeks, Lenten communal suppers, Diwali sweet-making classes, Eid celebrations, harvest festivals. Food and faith, together.

ONGOING
Elder Feeding Ministry

Trained congregation volunteers deliver weekly meals to elderly members who can no longer attend services. The visit is as important as the meal. Integrated with the ZHIP Elder Nourishment Programme for coordination and impact tracking.

How the Academy teaches at a Faith Hub

The Academy trainer at a faith hub adapts the curriculum to the congregation's dietary practices — halal, kosher, vegan, vegetarian. Cultural food knowledge from within the congregation is woven into the curriculum. Elders are invited to co-teach. The faith kitchen becomes the classroom. Certifications recognise the teaching that has always happened at the community table — and give it formal standing.

Community programmes at a School Hub

EVERY DAY — TERM TIME
Breakfast and Lunch Programme

Nutritious hot breakfast and lunch for every child — regardless of family income. Meals are freshly cooked, culturally diverse, and planned to meet the nutritional needs of growing children. ZHIP supports menu planning, food rescue integration, and volunteer training for kitchen support staff.

WEEKLY — ZHIP ACADEMY
After-School Cooking Club

Two age-differentiated cooking clubs per week: Seedlings (ages 5–10) and Growers (ages 11–16). ZHIP-certified teacher leads sessions adapted to the curriculum — garden-to-plate for younger children, food science and enterprise for older students. Every session produces something that goes home or is eaten together.

ONGOING
School Growing Garden

A dedicated growing space — raised beds, containers, or ground plots — maintained by rotating class groups throughout the year. What is grown feeds the school kitchen. ZHIP provides: site design, seasonal planting guides, teacher curriculum support, and a growing coach visit each half-term.

SCHOOL HOLIDAYS
Holiday Food Club

Children who receive free school meals do not stop needing food when school closes. The holiday food club runs for the first two weeks of each major school holiday — providing a hot meal, a growing activity, and a cooking session each day. Children take home what they cook.

MONTHLY — EVENINGS
Parent Cooking Workshops

Monthly evening cooking workshops for parents — building on what their children are learning in school, and extending food knowledge into the home. Run by the school's ZHIP-certified trainer, in the school kitchen, in the languages spoken by the school community.

How the Academy teaches at a School Hub

The Academy's children's curriculum is built around four laws: touch before taste, grow before cook, story before technique, pride before correction. Every session connects to something the child has grown, touched, or smelled. Teachers receive ZHIP curriculum integration guides connecting the growing garden and cooking club to maths, science, geography, and cultural studies. Children do not study food. They experience it.

Hub Type 2

🏫 The School Hub

Schools reach children at the age when lifelong food habits are formed. They already have kitchens, outdoor space, and a captive community of families. A ZHIP school hub turns the school into a food sovereignty engine for the whole neighbourhood — teaching children during the day, parents in the evening, and feeding the most vulnerable children year-round.

The school garden is the anchor. What children grow, they cook. What they cook, they eat. What they eat, they talk about at home. The knowledge travels from the school into the household — the reverse of how education usually works, and far more effective.

"The children who grew the tomatoes were the children who ate the salsa. The children who ate the salsa were the children who asked their mums to make it at home. That is how food culture changes — one tomato at a time."

— School Hub Programme Lead, Dallas
Hub Type 3

🏛️ The Community Centre Hub

The community centre hub is the most complete expression of a ZHIP hub — a dedicated space where all eight ZHIP programmes can run, the Food Skills Academy operates in full, and the community gathers across the full week. It is the kind of hub that, over time, becomes the food heartbeat of a neighbourhood.

Not every community starts here. Most start with one programme in one space and grow from there. But the community centre hub is the destination — the fully realised version of what food sovereignty looks like when a community commits to it completely.

"On Tuesday we feed sixty people lunch. On Thursday twelve adults learn to cook. On Saturday twenty families tend their plots. On the last Sunday of the month the whole street comes to eat together. That is what this centre is now. Three years ago it was a room that sat empty most of the week."

— Community Centre Hub Coordinator, San Antonio

The full weekly programme at a Community Centre Hub

MONDAY
Food rescue collection and pantry stocking

Volunteer drivers collect rescued food from supermarket and restaurant partners. Kitchen lead and pantry coordinator sort and store — fresh produce, shelf-stable goods, items for this week's kitchen sessions.

TUESDAY & THURSDAY
Community Hot Kitchen — lunch service

Hot meals for 30–60 people, cooked by the volunteer kitchen team. Culturally rotating menu. Open to all. Tables set, not trays queued. The Thursday service is also the practical teaching session for the Food Skills Academy — participants cook and learn simultaneously.

WEDNESDAY & FRIDAY
Community Food Pantry — open distribution

Three-hour dignity-first food distribution. Families choose from the displayed stock. Volunteers assist, welcome, and refer to other programmes. New family intake processed privately and respectfully.

THURSDAY EVENING — ZHIP ACADEMY
Foundation cooking course (6-week cohorts)

8–15 community members in a structured learning cohort — 2 hours, hands-on, taught in the community language by a ZHIP-certified trainer. Six-week Foundation leads to 8-week Intermediate, leads to Advanced. Ongoing, year-round, rotating subjects.

SATURDAY MORNING
Community growing sessions and seed library

Families tend growing plots, the growing coach gives seasonal guidance, children water and harvest, and the seed library opens for borrowing. Surplus harvest goes straight to the Tuesday kitchen.

SATURDAY AFTERNOON
Community market and skills exchange

Community members sell fermented goods, fresh produce, baked items, and food knowledge services they have developed through the Academy. Enterprise incubation in real time — with real customers and real money.

LAST SUNDAY OF MONTH
Community feast and cultural celebration

The whole neighbourhood invited. A different culture's food tradition celebrated each month. Elders guest-teach. Young people document. New families introduced. Fundraising for the community food fund through a suggested donation model.

Community programmes at a Healthcare Hub

ONGOING — CLINICAL INTEGRATION
Food Prescription Programme

Clinicians identify patients for whom diet is a primary factor in their condition — type 2 diabetes, hypertension, obesity, cardiovascular disease. They issue a ZHIP food prescription: a referral to the hub's food pantry (for fresh produce), the cooking course (for skills), and the growing programme (for long-term capability). Impact is measured and reported back to the clinical team.

WEEKLY — ZHIP ACADEMY
Condition-Specific Cooking Courses

Academy cooking courses adapted for specific health conditions — diabetes-friendly cooking, low-sodium cooking for hypertension, anti-inflammatory eating, postpartum nutrition, and eating well on a fixed income. Delivered by ZHIP-certified Healthcare Trainers, co-designed with the clinical team.

IN CLINIC
Food Insecurity Screening and Referral

Every patient appointment includes a brief food security screen. Patients experiencing food insecurity are referred — with dignity and without bureaucracy — to the hub's pantry, cooking class, or family food box programme. Community Health Workers are trained by ZHIP in the referral pathway.

MONTHLY
Community Health and Food Sessions

Monthly community sessions — open to patients and neighbours — combining health education with cooking demonstration. A clinician presents on a health topic (managing blood sugar, reducing salt, understanding nutritional labels) and a ZHIP trainer cooks a relevant meal. The food eaten at the session is the learning.

ONGOING
Postpartum and Maternal Nutrition Support

New mothers and pregnant women receive culturally appropriate nutrition support — family food boxes with postpartum-specific ingredients, a dedicated cooking cohort for new mothers, and connection to the growing programme so families can begin growing food together from the earliest stages of parenthood.

Hub Type 4

🏥 The Healthcare Hub

Diet is a factor in five of the ten leading causes of death in the United States. Most healthcare settings can diagnose the problem — but they cannot prescribe the solution because the solution is not a drug: it is a plate of food, a cooking skill, and a growing garden.

A ZHIP healthcare hub connects the clinical and the communal — bringing food access and food knowledge directly into healthcare settings, and connecting healthcare institutions to the food programmes happening in the community around them.

The healthcare hub is where the ZHIP Academy is at its most medically precise — courses designed specifically for people managing chronic conditions, taught in clinical settings, measured against clinical outcomes.

"We had been telling patients with type 2 diabetes to eat better for years. What changed was when we could show them how — in a kitchen, with their hands, with ingredients they could actually afford. Three months after the cooking course: average HbA1c down 1.2 points."

— Clinical Lead, ZHIP Healthcare Hub Partner
Hub Type 5

🏘️ The Neighbourhood Hub

A neighbourhood hub can be a housing association common room, a converted garage, a covered outdoor space, or a community garden with a small covered area. It serves the people who live within walking distance — literally the closest neighbours — and builds the kind of food community that used to exist naturally in every street.

Neighbourhood hubs are often the smallest hubs — and often the most intimate. They serve twenty families rather than two hundred. But the depth of community they build is disproportionate to their size. When the scale is small enough that everyone knows everyone, what gets built is not just a food programme — it is a neighbourhood.

Community programmes at a Neighbourhood Hub

Community Fridge and Sharing Shelf

A publicly accessible refrigerator and dry goods shelf where anyone can leave surplus food for anyone to take — no registration, no eligibility, no shame. Monitored daily by a volunteer. Becomes the daily rhythm of neighbourhood food sharing.

Shared Growing Space

A shared garden or allotment managed by resident families — with individual plots and shared growing of high-yield crops like courgettes, tomatoes, and herbs that are divided at harvest. A growing coach visits monthly.

Neighbour Cooking Nights

Monthly cooking evenings at a resident's home or shared space — rotating hosts, rotating cuisines. Each host cooks a dish from their family's tradition and shares the recipe. An informal but consistent community bond around food.

Elder Meal Train

A rotating schedule where neighbours take turns preparing and delivering a hot meal to elderly or housebound residents on the street. Coordinated through the ZHIP platform. Simple, consistent, and profoundly connective.

ZHIP Academy — Home Learning Cohort

A small cohort of 4–6 residents completing Academy modules together — meeting weekly in each other's homes, cooking together, supporting each other's progress. The trainer is a neighbour who has completed their trainer certification. Learning happens in the most natural possible setting.

Community programmes at a Food Enterprise Hub

ONGOING
Community Kitchen Incubation

Certified kitchen space available for rent by community food enterprises in off-peak hours — providing a commercial-grade production facility to food entrepreneurs who cannot afford their own. ZHIP-certified businesses get priority access and subsidised rates.

ZHIP ACADEMY
Food Enterprise Courses

ZHIP Academy food enterprise modules delivered in the hub — food business registration, food safety certification, recipe costing, pricing, packaging, market selling, and cooperative enterprise models. Practical, community-scale, and directly applicable to selling at the weekend market.

WEEKLY
Community Market

A regular market where community food enterprises and growing programmes sell directly to neighbours — ferments, baked goods, fresh produce, catering services, and food knowledge. Real commercial transactions. Real income for community members.

ONGOING
Enterprise Mentoring Programme

Experienced ZHIP-certified enterprise trainers mentor new food businesses through their first year — business planning, regulatory compliance, market development, and impact measurement. Cohort-based, so enterprises learn from each other as well as from their mentors.

ONGOING
Free Community Meal from Enterprise Hub

Every food enterprise hub runs a free community meal programme — funded by a percentage of the commercial kitchen revenue and the market stall fees. The enterprise hub generates income that feeds the community. The circular economy in its most visible form.

Hub Type 6

🏪 The Food Enterprise Hub

The food enterprise hub is where food sovereignty meets economic sovereignty. It is a community kitchen incubator, a market, a training centre, and a business advisory service — all in one space, all oriented around the development of community-owned food businesses.

The food enterprises that grow from these hubs are not just businesses. They are the long-term economic infrastructure of community food sovereignty — local production, local employment, local circulation of money, and local control of what the community eats.

The ZHIP Food Skills Academy's enterprise modules are most fully realised here — where the learning happens in a commercial kitchen, is tested at a real market, and is supported by a cohort of fellow community entrepreneurs building alongside each other.

"I started in the fermentation module. Six months later I had twenty regular customers for my kimchi. A year later I hired my sister. The hub gave me a kitchen, a trainer, a market, and a community that actually wanted to buy what I was making."

— Food Enterprise Hub Graduate, Austin
Starting your hub

Seven steps from first conversation
to first programme session.

1

Tell us about your community

Contact ZHIP through our online form, by email, or by phone. Tell us who you are, what space you might have access to, and what food-related challenges you see in your neighbourhood. This conversation shapes everything that follows — and takes about thirty minutes.

2

Meet a ZHIP Programme Advisor

A Programme Advisor meets with you — in person if you are in a ZHIP region, by video call if not. Together you identify the most important needs in your community, the assets you already have, and which programme to start with. We never prescribe. We design with you.

3

Complete hub leader and programme training

The Hub Coordinator and first programme leads (kitchen, pantry, growing) complete the relevant ZHIP training — practical skills, values and dignity principles, impact recording, and the ZHIP platform tools. Available in person or online. Takes two to three days in total.

4

Receive your starter kit and platform access

ZHIP ships your programme starter kit — kitchen equipment, pantry setup materials, seed library founding collection, and printed programme guides — and sets up your hub's account on the ZHIP management platform. Your Programme Advisor walks you through the tools in a two-hour onboarding session.

5

Recruit and brief your first volunteer team

With ZHIP outreach materials and your own community connections, you recruit your first four to eight volunteers. Everyone receives a hub induction covering dignity-first service, basic food safety, and the specific role they will play. Induction takes two hours and is run by the Hub Coordinator.

6

Launch your first session — with ZHIP support alongside

For the first three sessions of any new programme, a ZHIP Programme Advisor is available by phone throughout. For hubs in ZHIP launch regions, an advisor attends the first session in person. After each early session, a fifteen-minute debrief identifies what worked and what to adjust.

7

Apply for Verified Hub status at three months

After three months of consistent programme delivery, hubs apply for ZHIP Verified Hub status — which unlocks fundraising tools, grant access, family kit distribution, and connection to the regional hub association. Verification involves a light-touch impact review and a community governance check.
